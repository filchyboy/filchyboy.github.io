## Today's Update

Today was the close of a long arc. The Revyrie demo hardening and expansion work — everything under `revyrie-demo-hardening-and-expansion` — is now complete, archived, and ready for the September 23 demo. That's four tracks of work across payment lane resolution, conversational commerce, frontend checkout state management, and appointment payment — plus the integration sweep and final readiness disposition that ties them together. Calling it done required actually running every quality gate scoped to each track, then running the union sweep across all of them, so "done" here means tested and dispositioned rather than just coded and committed.

The organizing decision for all of this was the per-tenant demo payment lane resolver. The `CheckoutPaymentLaneResolver` contract determines whether a given tenant routes through the real Stripe lane or the fake-provider lane, and everything downstream — `TenantMerchantAccountLaneResolver`, the `CheckoutPaymentSessionProvider` binding, the explicit fake-provider merchant binding command — depends on that resolution being deterministic and tenant-scoped. I wrote an ADR (`ADR-0376`) early in the track to lock that decision down before building the rest of it, specifically because the alternative — each piece of the checkout stack making its own lane decision independently — would have made the matrix disposition tests meaningless. The matrix runner validates that the four disposition quadrants (approve, decline, abandon, recover) behave correctly across both lanes, and that only works if lane assignment is centralized and testable. The unit tests for lane resolution rules and the cross-tenant negative tests with counterfactual controls are there to prove the boundary holds, not just that the happy path works.

The conversational commerce track (`rdhe-t2-*`) was the most structurally complex. `StartConversationalPurchaseAction` and `ConfirmConversationalPurchaseAction` connect to the chat interface through the Interaction Runtime, but the interesting engineering was in the `PurchaseControl` model and `PurchaseControlRedemptionService` — specifically the idempotency and race handling. I ran concurrent confirmation tests against MySQL to verify that double-submits don't produce double charges, and tested stale, altered, replayed, and expired controls as distinct failure cases rather than collapsing them into a generic "invalid control" path. The distinction matters for the demo: a presenter needs to be able to show what happens when a control expires mid-flow, and that's only demonstrable if expiry is a real state rather than a catch-all. The `AwaitingConfirmation` state and its transitions were added specifically to make that visible. The receipt dedup key migration (`rdhe-t4-02`) reinforces this at the database level.

On the frontend, the checkout state management work (`rdhe-t3-*`) covered the cases that are invisible until something goes wrong: refresh rehydration in `CheckoutConfirmation`, lost-response handling, pending outcome display, and the stale-request guard in `MCPChatInterface`. The `useConversationalPurchase` hook owns most of that state machine, and the RTL tests cover refresh, lost response, pending, and decline-retry as separate scenarios rather than a single "error state" bucket. The `PurchasePreviewCard` component was added to give the agent-initiated purchase flow a visible confirmation surface before the user commits. Accessibility coverage ran through `jest-axe` for both the chat interface and the preview card — that's not optional for a demo where someone might be watching screen-reader behavior. The Cypress matrix specs (`BP-F`, `BP-S`, `CC-F`, `CC-S`, `AP-F`, `AP-S`, operator continuation, order fallback, appointment refusal and recovery) are the end-to-end evidence that the whole surface behaves coherently rather than just passing unit-level assertions.

The final steps were operational closure rather than new capability: rewriting the operations guide across bootstrap, lanes, reset, dispositions, abandonment, and audience sequence; updating the claims register and presenter script; archiving the plan. The `demo:runtime-manifest` command and its `make` target give whoever runs the demo a single command to verify that the runtime state matches the frozen manifest before going live. That's the piece I'm most relieved to have — the previous audit passes accumulated precisely because there was no deterministic runtime check, just manual verification against a checklist. Having `demo:record-rehearsal` write idempotent records means rehearsal runs are also auditable, which closes the loop on the evidence chain that Pass 12 was designed to complete.
