## Today's Update

The day was almost entirely about contracts — not in the legal sense, but in the software sense: specifying precisely what each subsystem will accept, what it will reject, and what it will guarantee to the thing calling it. That theme ran through everything from DSR export verification to compliance scheme enforcement to the decisions reminder workflow, and it's the kind of work that doesn't produce visible features but does determine whether features built on top of it will be trustworthy.

The DSR slice was the largest body of work and the most sequentially dependent. I worked through twelve discrete steps, starting from verifying signed guarded source packets and admitting reviewed guarded writer assessments, then building outward to package verification before fulfillment, governed export byte verification before settlement, and finally local disk read-back confirmation. The ordering was load-bearing: you can't verify a fulfillment response against an unverified package, and you can't verify bytes before you've established what "governed" means in this export context. Two behavioral contracts I'm most confident about coming out of this: the fulfillment route validation now uses a form request (so malformed route IDs are rejected at the boundary, not discovered mid-execution), and stale export download state is hidden on status error rather than shown in an inconsistent half-resolved state. That second one is a UI contract decision as much as a data decision — showing stale download state when status has failed would invite future operators to act on information that no longer reflects reality. The selectable native export refusal image also got pinned, which eliminates a category of test flakiness that comes from comparing against a non-deterministic reference.

Compliance took a different shape. The scheme policy enforcement work built a layered gate: exact scheme policy is enforced when category drafts are created, rechecked during category review, and gated again at activation. Three separate enforcement points for the same policy sounds redundant, but it isn't — each gate corresponds to a different actor and a different failure mode. A draft created before a policy change, reviewed after it, and activated without rechecking would sail through a single-gate design and produce a category that violates the scheme it claims to belong to. Persisting the scheme metadata as immutable alongside that was the right call; I'd considered making it a live reference, but a live reference means the compliance record's meaning can change retroactively as the scheme evolves. That's worse than the extra storage. The fixture restoration work (owner resolver, registry projection metrics, native category scheme fixtures) matters in a less interesting way — broken fixtures produce test noise that obscures real failures, and I'd rather have no test than a test that gives false confidence.

The decisions thread centered on the reminder workflow and its concurrency boundaries. Making reminder workflow fixtures due, qualifying lock races on admission, fencing stale outbox claims, qualifying case-lock authority, binding reminder claims to the original reviewer, rechecking reviewer tenancy, and safely pruning expired inbox items — these seven steps form a sequence that's essentially a concurrency correctness proof for the reminder path. The binding of claims to the original reviewer is the one I'd call out specifically: a reminder that can be claimed by any reviewer creates an implicit reassignment path that bypasses whatever authority the original reviewer held. That's a silent privilege escalation in a compliance-adjacent workflow, which is exactly where you don't want surprises. The pruning logic required care too — "safely" in the task title isn't decorative. An inbox prune that removes items concurrently with a claim admission could delete a claim mid-flight. The pruning now operates only on items that have passed their expiry window with no pending claim activity.

The privacy and compliance scheme work converged on a shared concern: immutability of category shape once a scheme is established. I proved catalog scheme and version shape, isolated principal audiences while retaining the canonical privacy entry, rejected cycles in stored catalog payloads, and used a tenant-bound category immutability fixture. The cycle rejection is straightforward to describe and genuinely tricky to test — you need a catalog graph that contains a cycle, which means setting up a fixture that deliberately violates what the system should refuse, then confirming the refusal fires at the right layer. The two `col-6038` catalog tests (graph cycles and category shape) were validation of the scheme successor contract work that's still in progress, which means those tests are running against behavior that isn't fully specified yet. That's intentional — tests written against a partial spec surface gaps — but I'll want to resolve the successor contract before treating those tests as authoritative.

Three items progressed but didn't close: the scheme successor publication contract, the governed self-service DSR export execution, and the D15 Postmark-only transactional email provider boundary enforcement. The email boundary work is probably the one I'm watching most carefully. Enforcing Postmark-only at the provider boundary rather than at the call site is the right architecture, but the boundary itself needs to be unambiguous before D15 can be marked done. The commerce and email refusal items that did close today — refusing other-purpose evidence at the provider boundary, refusing broad marketing grants for recommendations, refusing recommendations without purpose-market evidence — are all building toward the same constraint: the email provider shouldn't be reachable from a path that hasn't satisfied its purpose evidence requirements. When the D15 work closes, those refusals are what make the constraint enforceable rather than advisory.
