## Today's Update

Thirty-four units across thirteen feature areas sounds like a scattered day, but the thread holding most of it together was the same concern in different registers: making sure that what gets written, settled, or delivered is fenced by the right authority at the right moment, and that the evidence for those decisions is retained in a form that's auditable later.

The bookings work was the largest single cluster and also the most technically specific. I aligned new admission-basis cases with the suppression path they actually record — the gap there was that newly introduced cases were being handled by logic that assumed the old path's suppression behavior, which would silently produce wrong outcomes without an assertion failure to catch it. Alongside that, I made held notice storage record *why* the notice is held and expose that reason to authorized operators. That's a deliberate data model extension: the held state alone doesn't tell an operator whether the hold is a temporary governance condition or a permanent refusal, and conflating those two meanings would make the held queue opaque at exactly the moment an operator needs to act on it. I also wired refusal history writes to happen atomically with clearing events and stale governance — the sequencing constraint here is that a refusal which clears without atomically retiring the old governance record leaves a window where two conflicting states are simultaneously readable. The MySQL qualification for finance HTTP proofs closed a separate gap: those tests were running against a dialect that doesn't enforce the same integer overflow and constraint behavior, so green results were insufficient evidence of correctness on the production path.

The decisions and trust work was tightly coupled to the bookings layer. Fencing outbox settlement by active attempt and fencing reminder settlement by current authority are both about the same class of problem: preventing a settlement from completing against a stale or superseded record. The published-reminders-stay-pending rule pairs with that — a reminder that's been published but not yet settled needs to stay in a pending state rather than advancing, because advancing it would allow a settlement fence to be bypassed by a racing state transition. On the trust side, recording the admission basis directly on the policy decision (established by the engine, not inferred at read time) means that downstream readers don't have to reconstruct engine intent from circumstantial evidence. That's a consequential distinction for compliance: reconstructed intent is fragile when policy evolves.

The state machine remediation items I closed today were mostly evidentiary — reviewing the finance Gate 1 evidence, the appointment replay Gate 1 evidence, the current-authority async evidence, and the native browser recovery evidence, then recording the remaining agent/browser acceptance criteria. These reviews aren't rubber stamps; the Gate 1 bar requires that the evidence actually supports the claim being made, not just that the test passes. The appointment-gap-closure policy reconciliation (D13-A) is the one I'd call genuinely open: automatic delivery and mandatory Trust review are in tension in a way that doesn't resolve cleanly at the architecture level. I've recorded the decision that Trust review takes precedence over automatic delivery when both apply, but the implementation boundary — specifically where the delivery pipeline checks for an outstanding Trust review before proceeding — will need careful placement. Getting that wrong in either direction either defeats the Trust review entirely or creates a deadlock where delivery never proceeds.

The external-agent-surface preconditions work produced two pieces that belong together: completing receipt fields on the existing evidence stores, then adding asymmetric receipt signing and fixing evidence retention. The signing addition is the more consequential one. An evidence receipt that isn't signed can be repudiated — an external agent can claim the receipt doesn't reflect what actually happened. Asymmetric signing gives the system a non-repudiable artifact that survives any future dispute about what the agent acknowledged. The build items (aligning the v3 reader with the compact trace byte limit and binding retained epoch metadata to the final inventory) are quieter but matter for what comes next: the reader alignment means trace consumers won't silently truncate data that falls near the byte boundary, and the epoch metadata binding ensures the inventory reflects what was actually retained rather than what was expected to be retained.
