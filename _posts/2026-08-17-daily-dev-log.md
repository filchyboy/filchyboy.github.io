## Today's Update

Two feature sets dominated today, and they couldn't be more different in character. The appointment scheduling productization work was about making a booking surface safe to hand to a future operator. The compositional market analysis work was about retiring a decade's worth of accumulated multi-tenant debt and building an entirely new analytical engine on top of the cleaned foundation. Both finished. That's the headline.

On the scheduling side, I worked through the complete governed booking link lifecycle — active, paused, and revoked resolution — alongside the public booking authorization discovery and cross-tenant concealment policies. The authorization model here is more nuanced than it first appears: it's not just "can this caller see this booking link," it's "what operations are this caller's grants explicitly bound to, and does this request fall within that boundary." Getting that wrong is the kind of thing that looks correct in happy-path tests and fails in adversarial ones. I also built out the atomic slot hold layer, including the calendar owner and capacity acquisition, hold expiry release, and the indeterminate-provider reconciliation path for cases where a provider isn't assigned at hold time. Booking confirmation creates the commercial record and triggers the communication obligation outbox, and the old `BookingPayment` write path is gone — removed cleanly now rather than left to accumulate callers. The accessible public discovery, checkout, and confirmation surfaces are in. The reschedule swap for the first sellable path also landed. Fourteen distinct steps, but they form one coherent sequence: a user discovers availability, holds a slot, confirms, gets a commercial record, and receives confirmation. That's the atomic unit this feature needed to establish.

The compositional market analysis work is genuinely hard to summarize without making it sound like busy work, because the volume is real but so is the purpose. The first phase was a full cutover of the legacy Market Intelligence tenant storage model to Billing Account scope — every model, every job, every controller, every scheduled entrypoint. I inventoried the legacy tenant storage and entrypoints, determined the cutover ownership order, added the Billing Account column across roughly 19 tables, ran the deterministic backfill task, added tenant-consistency constraints to each model, proved cross-account isolation with counterfactual test coverage for every path, enforced the `NOT NULL` ownership constraints, and then retired the `BelongsToMiTenant` compatibility trait entirely. The trait retirement is the meaningful endpoint: it closes off the escape hatch. Any future caller that tries to reach for that compatibility layer will hit a hard failure rather than silently operating in an incorrect scope. The guard test against trait reuse is there specifically because these things have a way of creeping back in.

From there, the CMA work shifted into building the analytical engine proper — and this is where I'm less certain everything is in its final shape. Seven phases: the foundation (taxonomies, schema, models, and factories for every entity in the graph), the claim and evidence authoring layer with the deterministic recomposition run, market envelopes and requirement graphs, competitive subject and profile modeling, governance and publication lifecycle, capability combination and discovery, and finally the continuous intelligence layer with scheduled reanalysis and legacy finding import. The recomposition run is probably the most structurally interesting piece — it's designed so that a run's dependency manifest is frozen at dispatch time, retries operate against that frozen input rather than re-resolving, and the result set is atomic. That means a failed run and its retry are provably operating on the same data, which matters when you're trying to audit why an analysis reached a particular conclusion. The pilot also ran: the California yoga-studio market definition went through the full sequence from evidence registration through Mindbody offering snapshot, requirement graph authoring, coverage mapping, opportunity assessment, and a final adversarial recomposition that produced publishable reports. Having a live pilot record in the system is the difference between an engine that works in theory and one I can point at when a future operator asks what the output actually looks like. I'm not fully satisfied with how the publication supersession logic interacts with the expiry detection — there's an ordering dependency in the staleness Action that I think holds up but deserves a second look before this path sees real use.

The other pieces today were smaller but not throwaway: appointment workflow hardening on the bookings side, Meet delivery and operations export on appointments, scheduling review feedback addressed, analysis API request typing and run creation authorization on the market intelligence frontend, and the weekly review package for August 16. The ESLint and Jest coverage remediation trackers also got updated with new entries — those exist specifically so the remediation work doesn't become invisible between sprints. Today's session closed out two of the largest planned feature sets on the roadmap simultaneously, which narrows the remaining scope considerably. The analytical engine is built. The booking surface has a complete path from discovery to confirmation. What's left is integration, boundary hardening, and the front-facing surfaces that expose these capabilities to future operators.
