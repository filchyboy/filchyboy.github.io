## Today's Update

The MCP layer got the most attention today, and by volume it's not close. Seventeen distinct units landed there — contract enforcement, trust validation, worker lifecycle, lock contention isolation, and recovery sequencing. That's not a coincidence; MCP is the layer where the platform's principal authority model gets exercised most directly, and each of these pieces had to be proven independently before anything upstream could rely on them. The trust declaration work is worth explaining: I'm refusing malformed projection trust declarations and malformed native discovery trust contracts at intake, not inside the action path. That's a deliberate boundary placement. Pushing validation outward to the intake surface means the action path can assume clean contracts rather than defensively re-checking them at every step. The trade-off is that intake gets more complex — but intake complexity is far cheaper to reason about than defensive checks scattered through the interior. Alongside that, I locked in how source policy versions get retained in principal receipts, which is the mechanism that makes it possible to audit whether a consent decision was made against a stale policy version or a current one. Without that retention, the audit trail is incomplete by construction.

The agent-interface work ran parallel and fed into some of the same concerns. Rejecting undeclared UCP alias contracts and validating UCP response contracts are two sides of the same interface hygiene problem: if the contracts on both sides of the UCP boundary aren't formally validated, the interface becomes an implicit agreement that future callers will violate without knowing it. Binding private UCP reads to the current principal authority is the third piece — reads that escape the current principal scope are a correctness problem, not just a security one, because the projected data belongs to the wrong context. The schema refusal discovery admission closes the loop: when a schema refusal occurs, the discovery request itself needs to be admitted and traced, not silently dropped.

The checkout sequence was more mechanical but no less necessary. Enforcing terminal contracts before commit means the checkout path can't progress past a terminal state without having verified that the terminal conditions are actually met — that's a gate that's trivially easy to skip in early builds and painful to retrofit once payment flows are wired through. Binding terminal effects to the admitted store and rejecting store mismatches before payment claims are the complementary pieces: the store identity needs to be pinned at admission and then checked again before any payment claim goes out, because a mismatch at that point is a correctness failure that could result in a claim against the wrong store. I also restored the complete canonical payment quote fixtures here, which had drifted from the real checkout source.

The privacy-category-management thread (COL-6038) is still in progress, but it moved meaningfully today. The governed Commerce recommendation workflow now has its source ownership connected, the lawful-basis evidence is bound to purpose and market, and I'm proving four-input recommendation admission alongside provider refusal. What's still open is the checkout source review for recommendation field bindings — that review determines whether the existing checkout fields cover the recommendation surface or whether new bindings are required. That's not a small question, and I haven't looked at it carefully enough yet to know how much rework it implies. Everything else on COL-6038 will wait on that answer.

The dependency bumps (Vite 8.3.4 and 7.3.7) were security-advisory driven — not optional, just overhead. The forms, bookings, workspace, staging, events, governance-receipts, and build work were smaller in scope but each closed a real gap: recognizing disposed intakes end-to-end, removing a duplicate intake assertion that was creating false positives, tightening approval recovery error surfacing, requiring fresh SSH authentication on the staging recovery path, isolating delivery authority from ambient context, and typing the private build tracing plugin. The tracing plugin typing is one of those things that seems like housekeeping until you're debugging a build trace and discover the plugin was returning `any` everywhere — typed output is the difference between a usable trace and noise.
